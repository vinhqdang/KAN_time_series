RESPONSE TO REVIEWERS - REVISION 2
SPADE: spline additive-noise DAG estimation for interpretable nonlinear causal discovery in managerial decision support
Manuscript ARRAY-D-26-04878, Array

We thank the Editor and the reviewers for the second round of comments. Reviewer 1 judged that reproducibility, statistical consistency, comparative fairness, and the discussion of assumptions are now satisfactorily addressed, and asked for two further improvements: clearer exposition and stronger real-world validation with a more convincing demonstration of managerial applicability. Reviewer 3 requires no changes. Reviewers 2 and 4 have not yet commented on the revised version, so for them we restate how each of their original comments is handled in the current manuscript (section, table, and figure numbers refer to the revised manuscript). Results are reported as they came out, including where they do not favour SPADE.

REVIEWER 1  (paste the text below into the reply box for this reviewer's comment)
==========

REVIEWER COMMENT (summary): Reviewer 1 (Revision 1, September 18). "The authors have partially addressed the previously raised concerns but not fully resolved them: the issues related to reproducibility, statistical consistency, and comparative fairness have been substantially and satisfactorily incorporated ... however, the concerns regarding clarity of exposition are only partially improved, as the manuscript remains technically dense and difficult to follow, while the more critical issues of limited real-world validation, continued over-reliance on synthetic benchmarks, and weak demonstration of practical managerial applicability remain largely unaddressed, with real-data experiments still confined to a narrow financial setting that yields inconclusive or negative causal insights."

RESPONSE:
Thank you; both remaining points were fair, and we acted on both.

(1) Clarity of exposition. We rewrote the Method (Section 2) for a reader from information systems or management, and moved technical detail to appendices. Specifically: (a) Section 2 now opens with a roadmap and a plain-language account of SPADE (Section 2.1, with the architecture in Figure 1) before any equation; (b) a notation table (Table 1) collects every symbol; (c) each assumption and each proposition is followed by an "in plain words" paragraph; (d) the acyclicity theorem, the instantaneous-variant algorithm, and the choice of sparsity penalty moved to a new Appendix C, leaving Section 2 with the model, the objective, and the algorithm; (e) the Abstract and the list of contributions were rewritten. We also removed from Sections 1-5 the running account of how earlier drafts differed (for example, mentions of corrected numbers and retracted claims); that audit trail is now in one place, Appendix D, so the main text reads as a single argument.

(2) Real-world validation beyond finance. We added a new Section 3.8 that tests SPADE on three public, non-financial datasets where structure can be checked against domain knowledge instead of a labelled graph: daily bike-sharing demand (730 days, 12 variables), hourly bike-sharing demand (17,303 hours, 14 variables), and hourly Beijing PM2.5 air quality (41,543 hours, 14 variables). Variables are arranged in time tiers (calendar and lagged values, then weather, then demand); an edge from a later tier to an earlier one is impossible ("forbidden"), and a short list of mechanisms is domain-certain (for example, commuters ride on working days, and demand and weather are persistent). Our metric is the AUROC with which a method's edge scores separate the certain edges from the impossible ones (BK-AUROC; 0.5 is chance). The protocols, variable tiers, and metrics were fixed and time-stamped before any method was run on these data, no hyperparameter was tuned on them, and uncertainty comes from a block bootstrap. We compare against all eight baselines of the instantaneous benchmark (including SCORE and NoGAM). Results:
  - Daily bike sharing: SPADE has the highest BK-AUROC (0.810 +/- 0.061) and beats seven of eight baselines in all six resamples; against DAGMA-nonlinear (0.703 +/- 0.159) the difference is not statistically established (p = 0.22). The learned working-day effect has the expected sign in all six fits (fewer leisure riders, more commuter riders).
  - Hourly data, common 2,000-row sample: SPADE is not the clear winner. It ties linear NOTEARS (bike sharing 0.634 vs 0.649, p = 0.69; Beijing 0.669 vs 0.669) and beats the other methods in most or all resamples.
  - Hourly data, full series: SPADE leads linear NOTEARS in all three resamples on both datasets (bike sharing 0.688 vs 0.622; Beijing 0.742 vs 0.658), consistent with a flexible model needing more data than a linear one.
  - Limits we report: at hourly resolution the pre-registered edge-shape checks are not informative, because the edges concerned are almost entirely pruned; the temperature-to-casual-riders edge on daily data is also nearly pruned. BK-AUROC is partial ground truth, causal sufficiency is not guaranteed, and the full-series comparison covers only the fast baselines.

(3) Managerial applicability. Section 4.2 (new) shows how a planner reads the learned structure on the bike-sharing data: which relationships the model trusts, what the edge functions say in actionable magnitudes (switching a day from non-working to working shifts the learned contribution to leisure riders by -0.77 standard deviations and to commuter riders by +0.51, stable across resamples), and how BK-AUROC turns "the graph looks plausible" into a measurable audit. We keep the earlier caution about the financial panel (Sections 3.9.3 and 4.1): its levels-based graph is read as shared non-stationary co-movement, not as validated causal structure. We still stop short of causal-effect claims on all real data. Appendix D lists every correction made to the benchmarks during development.

REVIEWER 4  (paste the text below into the reply box for this reviewer's comment)
==========

REVIEWER COMMENT (summary): Reviewer 4 (initial submission, September 8). Six comments: (1) correct or clarify Figure 1; (2) level the forecasting comparison with normalization-matched baselines; (3) treat non-stationarity in the real financial analysis; (4) review statistical reporting (Table 3 Wilcoxon p-values versus caption; uncertainty for the main instantaneous-DAG results); (5) check consistency of hyperparameters, runtimes, indexing, and statements; (6) keep causal claims on real data conservative.

RESPONSE:
We addressed all six; the manuscript states each outcome, and we note where results did not favour SPADE.

(1) Figure 1. The reviewer was right: the figure contained a leftover residual-backbone box inherited from an earlier design that would break identifiability. Figure 1 now shows the deployed architecture (RevIN normalization, edge functions and adjacency, per-target additive sum, RevIN de-normalization) with no intervening network (Section 2.4).

(2) Forecasting. Every deep baseline is now also reported wrapped in the same RevIN module (Table 8; Section 3.6). Among normalization-matched models SPADE has MSE 0.029, behind TSMixer+RevIN (0.024) and PatchTST+RevIN (0.028), and ahead of N-BEATS+RevIN (0.030) and LSTM+RevIN (0.053). SPADE's 300 epochs were chosen on an inner validation split of the training folds only. SPADE is the slowest forecaster (357 s), so we make no speed claim for forecasting.

(3) Non-stationarity. We reran the financial analysis on log-returns (Section 3.9.3). The result is negative: with almost no one-step-ahead signal the learned graph becomes fully connected, so the sparse levels-based graph is read as shared non-stationary co-movement, not validated short-horizon causal structure. The Discussion, Contributions, Abstract, and Conclusion carry this caveat.

(4) Statistical reporting. The caption of Table 4 (formerly Table 3) now agrees with the Wilcoxon values and the text. Standard deviations were added to the instantaneous-DAG table (Table 2); NoGAM at d=20 rests on a single seed and is flagged as such. Hyperparameter selection for SPADE on that benchmark used twenty validation seeds disjoint from the reported seeds (Appendix B).

(5) Consistency. The runtime table (Table 6) now contains only widths measured under one protocol (d = 5, 10, 20, 50); a mixed-protocol d = 15 row was removed. A non-linear lagged-data generator that occasionally produced non-finite or extreme data is now screened (Section 3.3), and the group-lasso weight is width-dependent at d = 50 (Appendix B). Every correction, with cause and effect on the numbers, is listed in Appendix D.

(6) Conservative causal language. Real-data edges are described as candidate mechanisms throughout (Assumption 1, Sections 3.8-3.9, 4, and 5). The bike-sharing study scores agreement with domain knowledge and is not a causal-effect estimate.

REVIEWER 2  (paste the text below into the reply box for this reviewer's comment)
==========

REVIEWER COMMENT (summary): Reviewer 2 (initial submission, September 8). Comments: (1) novelty beyond combining existing pieces; (2) Propositions 1 and 2 need stronger arguments or weaker statements; (3) no experiment isolates the spline contribution (matched-capacity MLP edges), and SCORE/NoGAM are missing; (4) tightening: equal epochs versus equal budget, baseline tuning, collapsed neural baselines, RevIN fairness, the runtime jump between d=15 and d=20.

RESPONSE:
(1) Novelty. Section 1.2 states first what is not new (the component-wise bottleneck, shared with neural-Granger models and GC-KAN) and then what is: learnable per-edge B-spline mechanisms, a block-structured acyclicity constraint applied only to contemporaneous edges, and an experimental design that isolates which ingredient matters.

(2) Propositions. Remark 1 states that the assumptions (sufficiency, Markov/minimality, restricted non-linearity, stationarity) are posited properties of the data-generating process, not consequences of optimization, and that the proposition concerns the unregularized population target. Proposition 2 now states that the bottleneck is necessary but needs a sparsity preference for uniqueness; the group-lasso plays that role. Each statement is followed by a plain-words paragraph, and the longer arguments are in Appendix A.

(3) Spline versus MLP edges; missing baselines. We ran the requested ablation: identical architecture, optimizer, epochs, seeds, and group-lasso, with each B-spline edge (11 coefficients) replaced by a matched-capacity MLP edge (10 coefficients). AUROC is 0.889 for MLP edges and 0.902 for splines (Table 10); with three seeds we report point estimates and do not claim a tested equivalence. We conclude that the spline's distinctive value is the interpretability of the edge shapes, not a structure-recovery advantage over an equally sized MLP. SCORE and NoGAM were added to the headline benchmark (Table 2). SPADE reaches AUROC 0.947/0.926/0.915 at d = 6/10/20; it beats SCORE at every width and NoGAM at d = 6 and d = 20, but trails NoGAM at d = 10 (0.926 vs 0.955), while training 275-930x faster. NoGAM at d = 20 is one seed.

(4) Tightening. We report wall-clock time alongside epochs (Tables 6 and 8). Baselines run at their reference defaults; this and the possible reasons for the collapse of GraN-DAG and DAGMA-nonlinear are stated in Section 3.1. The forecasting comparison is normalization-matched (Table 8). The d = 15 runtime row was a mixed-protocol artifact and was removed.
