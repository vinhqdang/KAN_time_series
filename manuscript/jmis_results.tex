
\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate SPADE on (i) synthetic data with known ground-truth graphs, (ii) a
leakage-free forecasting protocol, (iii) a non-financial operations setting
with partial ground truth from domain knowledge (daily bike-sharing demand,
Section~\ref{sec:bike}), and (iv) real financial markets where no ground truth
exists. All code and configurations are released for reproduction, and every
number below is produced by the accompanying scripts rather than reported from a
single tuned run. Corrections made to the benchmarks during development are
catalogued in Appendix~\ref{app:corrections}.

\subsection{Datasets, baselines, metrics, and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Synthetic data.} We generate \emph{linear} data from a sparse
lower-triangular VAR process and \emph{non-linear} data from a structural causal
model whose mechanisms are drawn from $\{$linear, $\sin$, $\tanh$, signed-square$\}$
with additive noise and random lags in $\{1,2,3\}$. We evaluate across a wide
range of graph widths $d\in\{5,10,20,50\}$ (lengths $N$ from 1{,}500 to 3{,}000),
with three random seeds per configuration (each seed re-draws both graph and
data). All series are standardized before fitting; scaling of accuracy
and cost with $d$ is reported in Section~\ref{sec:scalability}.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare against \emph{genuine} implementations spanning
classical and recent methods. Classical/econometric: VAR-Lasso
\citep{Tibshirani1996}, PCMCI (tigramite, \citealt{Runge2019}), VAR-LiNGAM
(lingam, \citealt{Hyvarinen2010}), and a lagged-correlation threshold.
Differentiable DAG learning: linear NOTEARS \citep{Zheng2018} and GOLEM
\citep{ng2020golem}. \emph{Recent} deep temporal causal discovery: \textbf{Neural
Granger Causality} (cMLP, \citealt{Tank2021}, IEEE~TPAMI~2021) and
\textbf{NTS-NOTEARS} \citep{sun2023ntsnotears} (AISTATS~2023), both run from the
authors' vendored code. Within the KAN family: GC-KAN \citep{GCKAN2024} with
genuine B-spline edge functions, plus a \textbf{GC-KAN$+$ALM} ablation that adds
our acyclicity penalty to the GC-KAN backbone, isolating the DAG constraint's
contribution from that of the KAN edge functions. We attempted to include Rhino
\citep{Gong2023Rhino}, but its \texttt{causica} package requires Python${<}3.11$,
incompatible with our environment; we report this rather than omit it silently.
Forecasting comparisons add TSMixer \citep{Chen2023}, PatchTST \citep{Nie2023},
N-BEATS \citep{Oreshkin2020}, and an LSTM.

\paragraph{Baseline hyperparameters.} We run every baseline at its authors'
reference-implementation defaults (architecture width/depth, acyclicity-penalty
schedule, learning rate) rather than searching hyperparameters per dataset.
SPADE's own hyperparameters were chosen once, on synthetic validation seeds
disjoint from every reported seed (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} and
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), and then held fixed across experiments, so neither
side receives a per-dataset tuning advantage. We do not claim the baselines are
individually tuned to their best performance: an equally exhaustive search for
every baseline in every $(d,\text{regime})$ cell was outside our compute budget.
Where a baseline collapses (e.g.\ GraN-DAG and DAGMA-nonlinear in
Section~\ref{sec:instdag}), we can say only this: these methods read structure
from the input Jacobian of a generically sized MLP or normalizing-flow mechanism
trained with their default acyclicity schedule, and at these sample sizes that
read-out is high-variance. This is a plausible, not a proven, explanation, and a
wider baseline search is a natural direction for follow-up work.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Because thresholding confounds structural-ranking quality
with a chosen operating point, we report \textbf{AUROC} and \textbf{AUPRC}
(threshold-free) as primary metrics, and F1/SHD at each method's own best-F1
threshold---the identical selection procedure applied to every method, SPADE
included, so no method is advantaged. All causal metrics are computed only on
synthetic data with known ground truth; on real data we use stability-based
validation (Section~\ref{sec:real}). For SPADE we use the edge-probability
(summary-adjacency) readout; a coefficient-magnitude readout performed markedly
worse and is not used.

\paragraph{Forecasting protocol.} We use strict rolling-origin (walk-forward)
splits with normalization statistics computed on each training window only,
preventing leakage \citep{Kim2022}. SPADE is trained for 300 epochs at learning
rate $5\times10^{-3}$, an epoch count chosen on an inner validation split of the
training fold; the deep baselines (LSTM, TSMixer, PatchTST, N-BEATS), which we
found unstable at that learning rate, are trained for 80 epochs at
$5\times10^{-4}$. Because equal epochs are not equal compute, we report
wall-clock training time alongside MSE/MAE (Table~\ref{tab:forecast}). We report
one-step-ahead MSE/MAE averaged over the rolling folds and seeds.

\subsection{Headline: non-linear instantaneous causal discovery}
\label{sec:instdag}

SPADE's central strength is recovering \emph{non-linear} causal structure. We
test this directly on the classic instantaneous-DAG setting: i.i.d.\ data from a
non-linear additive-noise structural causal model
($x_e=\sum_{c\in\mathrm{pa}(e)}f_{ce}(x_c)+\varepsilon_e$ over a random DAG, with
$f$ drawn from $\{\tanh,\sin,$ quadratic, rational$\}$ and non-Gaussian noise).
Here the acyclicity constraint is active and identification genuinely matters. We
compare against linear and non-linear DAGMA \citep{Bello2022}, GraN-DAG
\citep{lachapelle2020grandag}, GOLEM \citep{ng2020golem}, a NOTEARS-MLP, and
linear NOTEARS \citep{Zheng2018}, the neural learners using their authors'
reference implementations, across widths $d\in\{6,10,20\}$ with five random
graphs at $d{=}6,10$ and three at $d{=}20$ (reduced there for compute budget).
We also include the two score-matching topological-ordering methods that
Section~\ref{sec:related} identifies as the theoretical basis for non-linear ANM
identifiability: SCORE \citep{rolland2022score} and NoGAM
\citep{montagna2023nogam}, via the authors' reference implementation
(\texttt{dodiscover}). Both output a binary post-pruning DAG rather than a
continuous score, so their AUROC/AUPRC come from that binary decision, an
inherent property of order-then-prune methods. NoGAM's kernel-ridge pruning is far
more expensive than every other method and scales roughly quadratically in $d$;
at $d{=}20$ one seed took over an hour, so NoGAM is reported at $d{=}20$ on a
single seed (flagged $^\dagger$ in Table~\ref{tab:instdag} and excluded from that
column's std), while SCORE keeps the same protocol as every other method.

\paragraph{How $\lambda_g$ was chosen.} The group-lasso weight is the only
hyperparameter we tuned for this benchmark. It was selected by a sweep on
twenty validation graphs (seeds $100$--$119$) that are disjoint from the
reporting seeds; $\lambda_g=0.01$ performed best, with $5\times10^{-3}$
statistically indistinguishable from it (Wilcoxon $p=0.39$) and $0.02$ clearly
worse. Because the sweep itself leaves a plateau of near-equivalent values, a
different reading of it could have chosen a value near $5\times10^{-3}$; we
report the value the procedure selected. The sweep script, its seeds, and the
history of this choice, including a seed-overlap error in an earlier version of
the script that we found and corrected, are in Appendices~\ref{app:repro}
and~\ref{app:corrections}. The acyclicity constraint is unaffected
($h(W^{(0)})\approx10^{-3}$ throughout).

\paragraph{The comparison in full.} Against the fixed-architecture,
gradient-based family SPADE's advantage is large: DAGMA-nonlinear
($\approx$0.59), NOTEARS-MLP ($\approx$0.61), and GraN-DAG ($\approx$0.52,
barely above chance) remain far behind, as do the linear learners
(DAGMA-linear $\approx$0.50, GOLEM $\approx$0.72) and linear NOTEARS
($0.70$/$0.79$/$0.88$), which SPADE beats at $d{=}6,10$ and ties at $d{=}20$.
Against the score-matching methods the picture is mixed rather than a clean
sweep: NoGAM attains AUROC~$0.917\pm0.077$/$0.955\pm0.058$/$0.906$ (single seed
at $d{=}20$) and SCORE $0.804\pm0.153$/$0.887\pm0.069$/$0.821\pm0.023$. SPADE
beats both at $d{=}6$ and $d{=}20$ ($0.915$ vs.\ NoGAM's single-seed $0.906$),
beats SCORE at $d{=}10$ ($0.926$ vs.\ $0.887$), and trails NoGAM at $d{=}10$.
SPADE trains roughly $532\times$ faster than NoGAM on average (2.7\,s vs.\
1422\,s per seed), and it yields inspectable edge functions
(Section~\ref{sec:splines}) that NoGAM's ordering-plus-pruning procedure does not
produce; even where NoGAM leads on AUROC, it does so at roughly $860\times$ the
compute and without an interpretable mechanism. The comparison is not perfectly
symmetric in tuning effort: NoGAM and SCORE were run at their defaults, without
the validation pass SPADE received, and retuning NoGAM was beyond our compute
budget. Because all series are standardized (Remark~\ref{rem:varsort}), neither
SPADE's advantage nor its $d{=}10$ shortfall can be a variance-ordering artifact.

\subsection{Lagged (temporal) causal discovery}
\label{sec:synthetic}

Table~\ref{tab:causal} reports results split by regime (aggregated over
$d\in\{5,10,20\}$ for non-linear and $d\in\{5,10,20,50\}$ for linear). On
\textbf{linear} structure SPADE is at or near ceiling (AUROC~$=$~0.999,
F1~$=$~0.985), matching the mature estimators (VAR-Lasso, GOLEM, PCMCI,
VAR-LiNGAM, all $1.0$) at every width tested including $d{=}50$, and well
above linear NOTEARS (0.86) and Neural-GC (0.71). On \textbf{non-linear} structure SPADE
attains AUROC~$=$~0.93 (F1~$=$~0.83), essentially tied with or ahead of every
other flexible neural/KAN learner we test---GC-KAN (0.91), GC-KAN$+$ALM (0.92),
and NTS-NOTEARS (0.91)---and now close behind, rather than substantially
trailing, the classical/conditioning-based methods (PCMCI~0.999, VarLiNGAM~0.99,
VAR-Lasso~0.98, GOLEM~0.99).

\paragraph{Benchmark validity safeguards.} The non-linear lagged generator
(\path{generate_nonlinear_scm}) permits cycles in its lagged summary graph and
includes an unbounded mechanism (a signed quadratic), so a minority of
$(d,\text{seed})$ draws diverge to non-finite or extreme-scale data. Training or
scoring on such a draw measures numerical failure rather than method quality, and
it affects method families differently: classical baselines raise an exception on
non-finite input, whereas gradient-based methods guard against non-finite loss and
then remain at their initialization. We therefore screen every draw for
non-finite values or $|x|>20$ and deterministically reseed
($\text{seed}+10^{4}k$, $k=1,2,\dots$) before any method sees the data; the
effective generator seed is logged with every result row. Only non-linear draws at
$d\in\{10,20,50\}$ were affected; linear data and $d{=}5$ never triggered the
screen. Results obtained before this screen existed are recorded in
Appendix~\ref{app:corrections}.

\paragraph{Width-dependence of $\lambda_g$.} SPADE's group-lasso sums a per-edge
norm over all $d(d{-}1)\times\text{max\_lag}$ candidate edges, so a fixed
$\lambda_g$ becomes a stronger penalty, relative to the reconstruction loss, as
$d$ grows. The default $\lambda_g{=}0.01$ works for $d\le20$ but over-regularizes
at $d{=}50$: on held-out validation seeds (142--144, disjoint from the reporting
seeds) linear $d{=}50$ AUROC is $0.78$ at $\lambda_g{=}0.01$ and $1.0$ for every
$\lambda_g\le0.003$. We use $\lambda_g{=}0.002$ at $d\ge50$ (documented in the
code, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) and obtain AUROC~$1.000$, F1~$0.993$, matching
GOLEM. In summary: on lagged \emph{linear} structure SPADE matches the best
baseline at every width $d\in\{5,10,20,50\}$; on lagged \emph{non-linear}
structure it is on par with the best flexible neural/KAN learners and close
behind, though not ahead of, the classical baselines (PCMCI, VAR-LiNGAM,
VAR-Lasso). SPADE's decisive advantage remains the \emph{instantaneous} regime of
Section~\ref{sec:instdag}.

\paragraph{A caveat on the non-linear lagged comparison.} PCMCI is run here with a
linear partial-correlation test (ParCorr) and still attains AUROC~$\approx$~0.999
on the non-linear generator. This suggests that, for edge \emph{presence}, the
generator's mechanisms (linear, $\sin$, $\tanh$, signed square: largely monotone
over the $O(1)$ range of the data) leave enough linear signal; AUROC on edge
presence therefore under-tests the recovery of non-linear \emph{mechanisms}, and
PCMCI's lead should not be read as evidence that linear methods recover curved
relationships. A non-linear conditional-independence variant of PCMCI (e.g.\ GPDC
or CMIknn) is the appropriate comparison and is future work.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.} Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
(Table~\ref{tab:causal_stats}, F1 over matched configuration/seed pairs) show
SPADE significantly \emph{outperforms} Neural-GC ($p<0.001$), NOTEARS
($p{=}0.001$), GC-KAN$+$ALM ($p{=}0.012$), and the correlation baseline
($p<0.001$), is statistically
\emph{indistinguishable} from GC-KAN ($p{=}0.050$) and NTS-NOTEARS
($p{=}0.063$); PCMCI, VAR-LiNGAM, VAR-Lasso, and
GOLEM (classical/conditioning-based and continuous-optimization linear baselines,
respectively) remain ahead ($p<0.01$) on this pooled linear$+$non-linear
comparison, though SPADE ties them exactly on the linear-only, $d{=}50$
configuration reported above. SPADE is thus a \emph{competitive}
causal-discovery method---at least on par with every flexible neural/KAN
baseline we test, including a significant edge over the KAN-Granger
GC-KAN$+$ALM variant---rather than a uniform state-of-the-art; we make
no such claim. Its
distinctive value (the instantaneous-DAG win, interpretable mechanisms, lag
attribution, coupled forecasting, and scaling to $d{=}50$) is developed in the
sections that follow.

\paragraph{Why the component-wise design matters.} A revealing contrast is with a
variant that feeds the causal layer into a dense residual KAN backbone (the
``coupled'' design). Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows this variant collapses to
chance (AUROC~$\approx$~0.5): the backbone minimizes forecasting error regardless
of the adjacency, so structure is not identified. Enforcing the information
bottleneck---each target predicted \emph{only} through its incoming edges---is
what makes SPADE's structure faithful, and the edge-level group-lasso is what
makes it sparse. GC-KAN, which shares the B-spline edges but lacks per-lag
resolution, sits below SPADE, and adding our acyclicity term to it
(GC-KAN$+$ALM) changes little on these purely-lagged graphs---confirming that the
gains come from the identifiable architecture, not the DAG penalty alone.

\subsection{Combined contemporaneous $+$ lagged structure (SVAR)}
\label{sec:contemp}

The headline experiment (Section~\ref{sec:instdag}) is purely instantaneous and
the lagged experiment (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}) purely temporal. Here we
confirm SPADE handles the \emph{combined} SVAR case: data with both a known
instantaneous DAG \emph{and} lagged edges, fit by the contemporaneous SPADE
variant (Section~\ref{sec:opt}) that predicts each variable from its instantaneous
parents (self-masked) and its lags, with the NOTEARS penalty on the contemporaneous
block $W_0$ dualized by ALM---a non-linear, KAN-based analogue of DYNOTEARS.

Table~\ref{tab:contemp} reports recovery of both blocks. SPADE recovers the
instantaneous DAG well at $d{=}6$ (AUROC~0.89) and---crucially, since the edges
are non-linear---\emph{substantially outperforms a linear instantaneous-NOTEARS
baseline} (0.89 vs.\ 0.67), while simultaneously recovering the lagged block
(AUROC~0.92) and driving the acyclicity residual to $h(W_0)\!\approx\!2\times10^{-3}$
(i.e.\ an essentially acyclic contemporaneous graph). Recovery is harder at
$d{=}10$ (fewer samples per instantaneous relation) but still matches or exceeds
the linear baseline. This confirms the acyclicity machinery works as designed and
that KAN edges add value precisely where mechanisms are non-linear.

\subsection{Scalability and runtime}
\label{sec:scalability}

The component-wise design is inexpensive: SPADE fits in seconds at $d{=}5$ and in
about fifteen seconds at $d{=}50$ (Table~\ref{tab:scale}, reporting parameter
count and fit time at $d\in\{5,10,20,50\}$ under the identical
trained-to-convergence protocol used throughout Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}).
Cost grows with $d$ as the $O(d^2 L)$ edge count increases. Its edge-wise spline
evaluation still makes it slower than the highly optimized classical baselines
(seconds vs.\ sub-second). SPADE is thus well suited to moderate-width systems
(tens of variables); the thousands-of-series regime would require vectorized
spline evaluation and low-rank acyclicity (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Forecasting}
\label{sec:forecast}

Table~\ref{tab:forecast} reports one-step-ahead forecasting on the 8-asset
financial dataset under the leakage-free rolling-origin protocol. Every deep
baseline (LSTM, TSMixer, PatchTST, N-BEATS) is reported both in its original
form and wrapped in the identical RevIN module SPADE uses (\texttt{+RevIN}
rows), so that the comparison separates architecture from normalization.
Normalization matters a great deal: without it the deep baselines degrade
badly (LSTM and PatchTST are worse than a naive predictor). Among
normalization-matched models, SPADE attains MSE~$=$~0.029, behind
TSMixer$+$RevIN (0.024) and PatchTST$+$RevIN (0.028) and ahead of
N-BEATS$+$RevIN (0.030) and LSTM$+$RevIN (0.053)---within a factor of
$1.2$ of the best. SPADE's epoch count (300) was chosen on an inner validation
split carved out of the training fold only, never on the test folds
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}); the baselines use their own, shorter schedules, so
equal epochs are not the relevant notion of a matched budget. The price of
SPADE's longer schedule is training time: at 357\,s per model it is the
slowest entry in the table, so we make no speed claim for forecasting. Its
distinguishing value here is that it is the only model that also yields an
interpretable, lag-resolved causal graph from the same training run, at a
narrow MSE margin from the best forecasters. A reader who weights raw MSE alone
would reasonably prefer TSMixer$+$RevIN or PatchTST$+$RevIN. (SPADE's speed
advantage holds in the causal-discovery comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:instdag}, a different task with a different budget.)

\subsection{Ablation and sensitivity}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates SPADE's design choices on non-linear synthetic
data. The decisive factor is the \textbf{information bottleneck}: replacing the
component-wise readout with a dense residual KAN backbone (the ``coupled''
variant) collapses recovery to chance (AUROC~$0.58$), because the backbone can fit
the forecast irrespective of the adjacency. The edge-level group-lasso trades
ranking quality for sparsity: removing it \emph{raises} AUROC on these small,
dense synthetic graphs (0.984 without the penalty vs.\ 0.902 with it), the usual
shrinkage trade-off of an $\ell_1$-style penalty. Its benefit is sparse,
thresholdable structure at larger, sparser $d$
(Section~\ref{sec:scalability}) and the tie-breaking role in
Proposition~\ref{prop:bottleneck}; we therefore do not describe it as neutral
for ranking quality.

\paragraph{Isolating the spline edges themselves.} The ablation
above isolates the bottleneck \emph{architecture} but does not, by itself, say
whether the B-spline edge parameterization is what drives recovery, as opposed
to the bottleneck-plus-group-lasso recipe it shares with prior component-wise
neural-Granger models (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). We add a fourth variant that
keeps the architecture, optimizer, learning rate, epoch budget, seeds, and
group-lasso weight identical and replaces each B-spline edge (11 coefficients)
with a matched-capacity $1\!\to\!3\!\to\!1$ tanh MLP edge (10 coefficients).
The result is AUROC~$0.889$/F1~$0.878$ for the MLP-edge variant versus
AUROC~$0.902$/F1~$0.867$ for the B-spline variant. We report the point
estimates rather than claim a tested equivalence: with only three seeds we
do not have the power to run a meaningful significance test (unlike the
paired Wilcoxon comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:causal_stats}, which use many
more matched configuration/seed pairs), and we did not run one here rather
than report an underpowered $p$-value as if it settled the question. What
we can say is that the gap between the two variants (0.013 AUROC) is small
relative to the seed-to-seed spread we observe elsewhere in this paper
(e.g.\ Table~\ref{tab:instdag}'s $\pm0.03$--$0.15$ AUROC across five seeds),
which is suggestive, not conclusive, evidence that edge parameterization is
not the primary driver of structure recovery here; the component-wise
bottleneck is the more clearly load-bearing factor
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}'s dense-backbone row collapses to chance, a much
larger and unambiguous effect), consistent with
Remark~\ref{rem:whatprop1claims} and the fact that
Proposition~\ref{prop:bottleneck} is architecture-agnostic.
SPADE's distinctive contribution is not a structure-recovery advantage from splines
per se, but (i) the \emph{interpretability} of the recovered edge shapes,
which a scalar MLP weight cannot provide (Section~\ref{sec:splines}), and (ii)
combining the bottleneck with an explicit NOTEARS acyclicity constraint, which
GC-KAN and neural-Granger baselines do not impose. The two edge types are also
not equally expensive per training step (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), so an
equal-epoch comparison alone does not equalize the training budget across edge
types---we report wall-clock time for exactly this reason.

\subsection{Non-financial real data: demand planning and air quality}
\label{sec:bike}

The financial panel of Section~\ref{sec:real} has no ground truth and, as
Section~\ref{sec:returns} shows, a weakly learnable one-step-ahead signal. To
test SPADE on managerial data where structure \emph{can} be checked, we use public
operational data with strong domain knowledge. Two properties are certain without
any labelled graph: the \emph{timing} of variables (yesterday cannot be caused by
today; weather and calendar are determined outside the system, so demand cannot
cause them) and a handful of mechanisms (commuters ride on working days;
warmer weather drives leisure riding; both demand and weather are persistent).
We sort variables into three tiers---pre-determined (calendar and lagged
values), weather, and demand---and call an edge \emph{forbidden} if it points
from a later tier to an earlier one, and \emph{positive} if it is one of the
domain-certain mechanisms. Our metric is the \textbf{background-knowledge AUROC}
(BK-AUROC): how well a method's edge scores separate positive edges from
forbidden ones ($0.5$ is chance). It is partial ground truth, not a full graph,
and it rewards methods for learning the structure that managers already know to
be true before trusting them on structure they do not.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Every protocol below was written and committed before any
method was run on the data (\path{scripts/realworld_bike_sharing.py},
\path{scripts/realworld_large.py}), no hyperparameter was tuned on these data
(SPADE uses its synthetic-benchmark settings; baselines use theirs), and
uncertainty comes from a moving-block bootstrap (six resamples; the Wilcoxon
test over six pairs cannot go below $p=0.031$). We use three datasets:
\emph{daily} Capital Bikeshare demand \citep{FanaeeT2014} ($n{=}730$ days,
$d{=}12$: workday indicator, year, lagged and current temperature, humidity, wind,
casual and registered riders; 41 forbidden and 7 positive edges);
\emph{hourly} Capital Bikeshare demand (same source, $n{=}17{,}303$ hourly
records, $d{=}14$, adding the hour of day; 9 positive edges); and \emph{hourly Beijing
PM2.5} \citep{Liang2015} ($n{=}41{,}543$, $d{=}14$: weather variables,
hour-of-day and month cycles, lagged values, and PM2.5; 10 positive edges
including ``wind speed reduces PM2.5'').

\subsubsection{Daily bike-sharing demand}
Table~\ref{tab:bike_daily} shows the result. SPADE obtains the highest BK-AUROC
($0.810\pm0.061$) and the fewest forbidden edges among the non-linear methods
that return a continuous score. It beats seven of the eight baselines in every
one of the six resamples ($p=0.031$, the smallest value possible with six pairs).
The exception is DAGMA-nonlinear ($0.703\pm0.159$), which SPADE beats in four of six
resamples ($p=0.22$): the difference is not statistically established at this
sample size. The order-then-prune methods SCORE and NoGAM return roughly 54 edges
of which about 19 are forbidden (at $d{=}12$ there are 132 possible edges), and
GOLEM and linear DAGMA score below chance, i.e.\ they prefer impossible edges to
domain-certain ones.

\subsubsection{Larger hourly datasets: sample size matters}
The daily study is small, so we repeat it on two much larger hourly datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:bike_hourly} and Table~\ref{tab:beijing}). Two settings are
reported. In the \emph{common-sample} comparison every method, including the
cubic-cost kernel method NoGAM, receives the same $2{,}000$-row block-bootstrap
sample in each of six resamples. In the \emph{full-series} check, SPADE and the
fast baselines are run on the entire series (Table~\ref{tab:large_scale}); the
slow methods cannot be run there.

On the common sample SPADE is \emph{not} the clear winner. On hourly bike sharing
it scores $0.634\pm0.067$ against $0.649\pm0.045$ for linear NOTEARS (SPADE ahead
in three of six resamples, $p=0.69$), and on Beijing PM2.5 the two tie at
$0.669$ ($0.095$ versus $0.055$ standard deviation). SPADE does beat every other
method on bike sharing (five of six resamples against GOLEM, $p=0.062$;
all six resamples against the rest, $p=0.031$). On Beijing it beats the
non-linear methods DAGMA-nonlinear ($p=0.44$) and NoGAM ($p=0.16$) in a majority
of resamples without that being statistically established, and beats GOLEM,
GraN-DAG, NOTEARS-MLP, SCORE, and linear DAGMA in all six. Linear NOTEARS is a
strong baseline here because lag-1 persistence dominates hourly data.

On the full series, however, SPADE leads in all three resamples on both datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:large_scale}): $0.688\pm0.007$ against $0.622\pm0.019$ for linear
NOTEARS on bike sharing ($n{=}17{,}303$), and $0.742\pm0.046$ against
$0.658\pm0.041$ on Beijing ($n{=}41{,}543$). A spline edge has many more
parameters than a linear one, so it needs more data to separate weak real edges
from noise; this is consistent with SPADE's modest result at $n{=}2{,}000$ and its
lead at full size. With three resamples we do not run a significance test, and
we did not tune SPADE on these data.

\paragraph{Edge shapes at hourly resolution.} The pre-registered shape checks do
not give usable evidence on the hourly data. The working-day edges (bike
sharing) and the wind-speed$\to$PM2.5 edge (Beijing) have the expected sign in
17 of 18 fits, but they are almost entirely pruned (importance $\approx0$,
median rank $85$ to $136$ of $182$ possible edges), so agreement in sign is not
evidence. A plausible reason is that at hourly resolution the lagged value of
each variable explains almost all of the signal, leaving little for the
group-lasso to keep among contemporaneous edges; we have not verified this.

\paragraph{Caveats (all three datasets).} BK-AUROC measures agreement with known
structure only; it does not validate the neutral edges, and a method could score
well by learning only persistence. Causal sufficiency is not guaranteed
(holidays, events, fleet size, emissions sources and policy are unobserved),
the daily study is short ($n{=}730$) and its working-day variable is binary, which limits what a spline can show, and none of these results are causal-effect estimates.

\paragraph{Lag attribution.} On synthetic data with \emph{known} lags, SPADE's
contribution-weighted lag estimate is accurate---mean absolute error 0.43 trading
days, correctly separating lag-1 from lag-3 edges. On the financial \emph{price
levels}, by contrast, the estimated lags concentrate near the middle of the
window ($\approx$3 days), reflecting the near-integrated (random-walk-like)
dynamics of prices where all recent lags are similarly predictive---consistent
with the non-stationarity concern; lag attribution is therefore most informative
on stationary or differenced series.

\subsubsection{Robustness to a stationary representation: a candid negative
result on returns}
\label{sec:returns}
A natural concern is that price levels are non-stationary. What happens if the causal analysis is redone on a genuinely stationary
representation (log-returns) instead of price levels? We reran the identical
pipeline (\path{scripts/extract_real_causal.py --returns}, same
architecture, hyperparameters, window, and three rolling folds) on daily
log-returns of the same eight assets. The honest answer is a negative result,
and we report it rather than the more flattering levels-based graph alone.
Validation MSE on returns is $0.51$/$0.79$/$0.98$ across the three folds---close
to or above the unit variance of the standardized target, i.e.\ barely better
than predicting the unconditional mean---consistent with daily returns on
liquid, actively-traded assets being close to informationally efficient and
largely unpredictable one day ahead. With essentially no predictive signal for
the group-lasso to concentrate on, the learned graph does not degenerate to
sparsity; it degenerates the \emph{other} way, to a fully-connected,
undifferentiated graph (edge density~$1.00$ in every fold, and consequently a
rolling-window Jaccard stability of exactly $1.00$---perfect but meaningless,
since a graph that is always everything is trivially identical to itself
across folds). This is not a software bug: it is what a forecasting-based
structure-learning method should honestly report when the forecasting problem
itself carries near-zero signal. Two conclusions follow, and we state both
plainly. First, this is itself evidence \emph{for} the non-stationarity concern: it
shows the sparse, stable graph reported on price levels
(Sections~\ref{sec:real}--\ref{sec:stability}) most plausibly reflects shared
non-stationary trends and slower co-movement across these assets, not
validated short-horizon (one-day) causal dependence---the levels-based graph
should be read with that caveat, not as if it survived a stationarity check it
did not. Second, it delimits SPADE's applicability honestly: forecasting-based
causal discovery of the kind SPADE performs requires a data regime where
one-step-ahead structure is genuinely learnable; at daily granularity on
efficiently-priced liquid assets, that regime may not hold, and a coarser
horizon, an intraday frequency, or exogenous instruments would be needed to
say more. We did not have a positive stationary-representation result to
report, and choose to say so rather than not run the check.
\label{sec:stability}
We estimate the graph on three rolling windows and measure the mean off-diagonal
Jaccard overlap of the binarized graphs (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}), obtaining
\textbf{0.31}. To test whether this exceeds chance---we compare against a permutation null of random graphs matched to each
fold's edge count. The observed stability is \textbf{4.7$\times$} the null mean
(0.066) with permutation $p<0.0001$: the learned structure is far more consistent
across time than random graphs of the same density, i.e.\ it reflects persistent
dependencies rather than noise.

\subsubsection{Validation beyond stability: time-reversal and regime shifts}
\label{sec:validation}
Stability shows consistency, not correctness. We add two stronger checks.

\paragraph{Time-reversal placebo.} A genuine temporal causal method should recover
the true (forward) graph far better from forward data than from time-reversed
data; invariance to reversal would indicate spurious correlation. On non-linear
synthetic data ($d{=}10$, 3 seeds), SPADE's recovery AUROC drops from
\textbf{0.94} (forward) to \textbf{0.64} (reversed)---a 0.30 gap---confirming that
it exploits temporal precedence rather than symmetric association.

\paragraph{Ground-truth structural change (intervention).} The most direct
test---does SPADE recover the \emph{expected change} in causal
structure?---requires a known change. We concatenate two SVAR
regimes with \emph{different} graphs at a known break ($t{=}1500$) and slide
SPADE across the series (Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}A). The graph-dissimilarity
signal between adjacent windows peaks around the break, and SPADE recovers the
correct regime graph on each side (AUROC~0.89 before, 1.00 after). Thus SPADE
detects the intervention \emph{and} recovers the two distinct structures---not
merely a stable graph.

\paragraph{Event-anchored regime analysis (real data, illustrative).} Applying the
same sliding-window analysis to the financial panel
(Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}B) surfaces structural shifts during the 2020 COVID
and 2021--2022 rate-hike eras; splitting at the 2022 rate-hike onset, the graph
changes in an economically coherent direction (post-2022, gold becomes a
downstream hub as flight-to-safety links appear). We stress this is
\emph{illustrative}: the rigorous, placebo-controlled version below shows real-data
single-event attribution is only marginal, so the clean intervention evidence is
the synthetic experiment.

\paragraph{Documented policy shock on real macro data (a candid negative result).}
To test recovery of a documented real-world intervention, we assembled a real
macro panel from FRED (2016--2026) that includes the \emph{actual policy
instrument}---the effective Federal Funds rate---alongside 2Y/3M/10Y Treasury
yields, the broad US-dollar index, the S\&P~500, WTI, and the VIX (stationary
representation: rates/yields/VIX differenced, price indices in log-returns). The
documented shock is the March~2022 Fed rate-hike liftoff. We report the outcome
honestly, because it is instructive:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Methodological (confirmed).} On daily \emph{price levels} the
    learned structure is degenerate (the dollar index carries $<$1\% of outgoing
    influence, swamped by co-trending non-stationarity); a stationary
    representation is necessary, consistent with the non-stationarity concern above.
    \item \emph{Change magnitude (null).} A placebo-controlled, symmetric-window
    test finds the 2022 liftoff produces a structural change no larger than
    typical placebo dates (dissimilarity 0.80 vs.\ placebo mean 0.75,
    $p{=}0.43$).
    \item \emph{Direction (contrary to a naive prior).} The interest-rate block's
    causal out-influence \emph{decreases} across the shock (0.38 $\to$ 0.20)
    rather than increasing; inspection suggests the pre-2022 window is dominated
    by the 2020 COVID rate shock, when Fed actions drove markets most strongly.
\end{itemize}
We draw a deliberate, honest conclusion: on purely \emph{observational} macro
data, SPADE's graph does not cleanly recover documented interventions in a
direction simple economic priors predict, and single-event attribution is not
statistically distinguishable from chance. This is a genuine limitation of
observational causal discovery (not specific to SPADE), and it is precisely why
we anchor the intervention claim on the \emph{controlled synthetic} experiment
(Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}A), where the ground-truth change is known and SPADE
recovers it cleanly. We prefer to report this null candidly rather than
over-interpret a learned macro graph.
