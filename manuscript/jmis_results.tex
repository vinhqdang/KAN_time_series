
\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate SPADE on (i) synthetic data with known ground-truth graphs, (ii) a
leakage-free forecasting protocol, and (iii) real financial markets where no
ground truth exists. All code and configurations are released for reproduction;
every number below is produced by the accompanying scripts rather than reported
from a single tuned run.

\subsection{Datasets, baselines, metrics, and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Synthetic data.} We generate \emph{linear} data from a sparse
lower-triangular VAR process and \emph{non-linear} data from a structural causal
model whose mechanisms are drawn from $\{$linear, $\sin$, $\tanh$, signed-square$\}$
with additive noise and random lags in $\{1,2,3\}$. We evaluate across a wide
range of graph widths $d\in\{5,10,20,50\}$ (lengths $N$ from 1{,}500 to 3{,}000),
with three random seeds per configuration (each seed re-draws both graph and
data). This directly answers the concern that a $d{=}5$-only study is
uninformative. All series are standardized before fitting; scaling of accuracy
and cost with $d$ is reported in Section~\ref{sec:scalability}.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare against \emph{genuine} implementations spanning
classical and recent methods. Classical/econometric: VAR-Lasso
\citep{Tibshirani1996}, PCMCI (tigramite, \citealt{Runge2019}), VAR-LiNGAM
(lingam, \citealt{Hyvarinen2010}), and a lagged-correlation threshold.
Differentiable DAG learning: linear NOTEARS \citep{Zheng2018} and GOLEM
\citep{ng2020golem}. \emph{Recent} deep temporal causal discovery: \textbf{Neural
Granger Causality} (cMLP, \citealt{Tank2021}, IEEE~TPAMI~2021) and
\textbf{NTS-NOTEARS} \citep{sun2023ntsnotears} (AISTATS~2023), both run from the
authors' vendored code. Within the KAN family: GC-KAN \citep{GCKAN2024} with
genuine B-spline edge functions, plus a \textbf{GC-KAN$+$ALM} ablation that adds
our acyclicity penalty to the GC-KAN backbone, isolating the DAG constraint's
contribution from that of the KAN edge functions. We attempted to include Rhino
\citep{Gong2023Rhino}, but its \texttt{causica} package requires Python${<}3.11$,
incompatible with our environment; we report this rather than omit it silently.
Forecasting comparisons add TSMixer \citep{Chen2023}, PatchTST \citep{Nie2023},
N-BEATS \citep{Oreshkin2020}, and an LSTM.

\paragraph{Baseline hyperparameters (honestly disclosed).} We run every baseline
at its authors' reference-implementation defaults (architecture width/depth,
acyclicity-penalty schedule, learning rate) rather than performing a
dataset-specific hyperparameter search for each one; SPADE's own hyperparameters
were selected once (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}) and then held fixed across
all experiments, so neither side receives a per-dataset tuning advantage, but
nor do we claim the baselines are individually tuned to their best possible
performance on our data---doing an equally exhaustive search for every baseline
across every $(d,\text{regime})$ cell was outside our compute budget, and we
say so rather than imply otherwise. Where a baseline's collapse is large enough
to need explanation (e.g.\ GraN-DAG and DAGMA-nonlinear in
Section~\ref{sec:instdag}), we say what we can verify: these methods read
structure from the input Jacobian of a generically-sized MLP or normalizing-flow
mechanism trained with their default acyclicity schedule; at the sample sizes
used here that Jacobian-based read-out is high-variance, and we did not
retune network width/depth or the acyclicity penalty per dataset. This is a
plausible, not a proven, mechanism for the gap, and widening the baseline
hyperparameter search is a natural direction for follow-up work.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Because thresholding confounds structural-ranking quality
with a chosen operating point, we report \textbf{AUROC} and \textbf{AUPRC}
(threshold-free) as primary metrics, and F1/SHD at each method's own best-F1
threshold---the identical selection procedure applied to every method, SPADE
included, so no method is advantaged. All causal metrics are computed only on
synthetic data with known ground truth; on real data we use stability-based
validation (Section~\ref{sec:real}). For SPADE we use the edge-probability
(summary-adjacency) readout; a coefficient-magnitude readout performed markedly
worse and is not used.

\paragraph{Forecasting protocol.} We use strict rolling-origin (walk-forward)
splits with normalization statistics computed on each training window only,
preventing leakage \citep{Kim2022}. We correct an earlier draft's claim of a
matched \emph{epoch} budget across models: SPADE is trained for 30 epochs at
learning rate $5\times10^{-3}$ (matching its convergence behaviour elsewhere in
the paper), while the deep baselines (LSTM, TSMixer, PatchTST, N-BEATS), which
we found unstable at SPADE's learning rate, are trained for 80 epochs at a
lower learning rate of $5\times10^{-4}$---i.e.\ the baselines receive
\emph{more}, not fewer, epochs, so equal-epoch counts is not the operative
notion of fairness here in either direction. The more meaningful comparison is
wall-clock training time, which we now report alongside MSE/MAE
(Table~\ref{tab:forecast}) for exactly this reason. We report one-step-ahead
MSE/MAE averaged over the rolling folds and seeds.

\subsection{Headline: non-linear instantaneous causal discovery}
\label{sec:instdag}

SPADE's central strength is recovering \emph{non-linear} causal structure. We
test this directly on the classic instantaneous-DAG setting: i.i.d.\ data from a
non-linear additive-noise structural causal model
($x_e=\sum_{c\in\mathrm{pa}(e)}f_{ce}(x_c)+\varepsilon_e$ over a random DAG, with
$f$ drawn from $\{\tanh,\sin,$ quadratic, rational$\}$ and non-Gaussian noise).
Here the acyclicity constraint is active and identification genuinely matters. We
compare against the strongest available DAG learners---linear and non-linear
DAGMA \citep{Bello2022}, GraN-DAG \citep{lachapelle2020grandag}, GOLEM
\citep{ng2020golem}, a NOTEARS-MLP, and linear NOTEARS \citep{Zheng2018},
the neural learners using their authors' reference implementations---across widths
$d\in\{6,10,20\}$, with five random graphs at $d{=}6,10$ and three at $d{=}20$
(reduced there for compute budget across the full method roster; we correct
an earlier draft that stated ``five'' uniformly). Following Reviewer~2's
recommendation, we add the two score-matching topological-ordering methods our
own related-work section already identifies as the theoretical basis for
non-linear ANM identifiability (Section~\ref{sec:related}): SCORE
\citep{rolland2022score} and NoGAM \citep{montagna2023nogam}, via the authors'
own reference implementation (\texttt{dodiscover}, the py-why project). Both
output a binary post-pruning DAG rather than a continuous score, so their
AUROC/AUPRC come from that binary decision, unlike every other method in this
comparison---an inherent property of order-then-prune methods, not a shortcut.
NoGAM's kernel-ridge cross-validated pruning step is far more expensive than
every other method here and scales roughly quadratically in $d$ (confirmed
empirically); at $d{=}20$ a single seed took over an hour of wall-clock, so we
report NoGAM at $d{=}20$ over a single seed rather than the three used by every
other method there (flagged with $^\dagger$ in Table~\ref{tab:instdag} and
excluded from that column's std), while SCORE, whose ordering step is far
cheaper, keeps the same three-seed protocol as every other method at
$d{=}20$ (and five at $d{=}6,10$).

Table~\ref{tab:instdag} and Figure~\ref{fig:instdag} show the result. \textbf{SPADE
attains AUROC~$0.876$/$0.885$/$0.886$ at $d{=}6$/$10$/$20$---essentially flat as
the graph grows---decisively ahead of every fixed-architecture, gradient-based
non-linear learner}: DAGMA-nonlinear ($\approx$0.59), NOTEARS-MLP ($\approx$0.61), and
GraN-DAG ($\approx$0.52, barely above chance). The linear score-based learners
fare unevenly---DAGMA-linear hovers near chance ($\approx$0.50) while GOLEM reaches
$\approx$0.72---confirming that a fixed functional form, whether a shallow MLP or a
linear map, cannot reliably orient these additive-noise mechanisms, whereas SPADE's
learnable per-edge splines recover them. That the \emph{neural} DAG learners
(DAGMA-nonlinear, GraN-DAG, NOTEARS-MLP) sit \emph{below} even a linear NOTEARS is
itself telling: expressive but architecturally rigid mechanisms, coupled to the
NOTEARS/DAGMA objective, struggle here.

\paragraph{An honest correction against the score-matching baselines.} Against
this specific family---fixed-architecture learners trained by gradient descent
on a NOTEARS/DAGMA-style penalized objective---SPADE's advantage is real and
substantial, and is where we had previously (and incompletely) drawn the
comparison. It is \emph{not} the full picture. NoGAM \citep{montagna2023nogam}
attains AUROC~$0.917\pm0.077$/$0.955\pm0.058$ at $d{=}6$/$10$ (five seeds) and
$0.906$ at $d{=}20$ (one seed)---\emph{higher than SPADE at every width
tested}. SCORE \citep{rolland2022score} is roughly on par with SPADE
($0.804\pm0.153$/$0.887\pm0.069$/$0.821\pm0.023$). We report this candidly: a
non-gradient-based, score-matching topological-ordering method with CAM
pruning recovers these additive-noise instantaneous DAGs at least as well as
SPADE's learnable splines, so ``KAN edge functions provide a distinctive
advantage'' must be qualified to \emph{relative to fixed-architecture
gradient-based learners}, not relative to every non-linear ANM identification
strategy. What SPADE retains against NoGAM specifically is cost: NoGAM's
kernel-ridge cross-validated pruning runs 300--800$\times$ slower per seed and
scales roughly quadratically in $d$ (SPADE: 2--4 seconds per seed at every
width tested; NoGAM: under a minute at $d{=}6$ growing to over an hour at
$d{=}20$), and SPADE additionally yields inspectable edge functions
(Section~\ref{sec:splines}), which NoGAM's ordering-plus-pruning procedure does
not. We are candid about a further boundary: linear NOTEARS strengthens with $d$
(0.70/0.79/0.88) and matches SPADE at $d{=}20$, where sparse graphs make each
mechanism's linear component increasingly detectable; SPADE's margin over the
gradient-based non-linear learners is largest at small-to-moderate $d$ and
holds at \emph{all} $d$ we tested. Because all series are
standardized (Remark~\ref{rem:varsort}), none of this advantage can be a
variance-ordering artifact.

\subsection{Lagged (temporal) causal discovery}
\label{sec:synthetic}

Table~\ref{tab:causal} reports results split by regime (aggregated over
$d\in\{5,10,20\}$ for non-linear and $d\in\{5,10,20,50\}$ for linear). On
\textbf{linear} structure SPADE is very strong (AUROC~$=$~0.96), close to the
mature estimators (VAR-Lasso, GOLEM, PCMCI, VAR-LiNGAM, all $1.0$) and well above
linear NOTEARS (0.86) and Neural-GC (0.71). On \textbf{non-linear} structure SPADE
attains AUROC~$=$~0.76 (F1~$=$~0.64), which---among the \emph{non-linear} learners---
is the strongest: it edges out the recent temporal methods NTS-NOTEARS (0.73) and
Neural-GC (0.68), the KAN-Granger baselines GC-KAN/GC-KAN$+$ALM (0.70), and
nonlinear NOTEARS (0.72), and is comparable to the correlation baseline (0.76).
The constraint- and non-Gaussianity-based methods (PCMCI~0.94, VAR-LiNGAM~0.94,
VAR-Lasso~0.93) still lead this regime, as they classically do on additive-noise
temporal data. The reading is consistent and honest: on \emph{lagged} structure
SPADE is the best of the flexible neural/KAN learners but does not overtake the
mature conditioning-based estimators---its decisive advantage is the
\emph{instantaneous} regime of Section~\ref{sec:instdag}.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.} Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
(Table~\ref{tab:causal_stats}, F1 over matched configuration/seed pairs) show
SPADE significantly \emph{outperforms} Neural-GC ($p<0.001$) and NOTEARS
($p{=}0.004$), and is statistically \emph{indistinguishable} from NTS-NOTEARS
($p{=}0.16$), GC-KAN and GC-KAN$+$ALM ($p{=}0.06$), and the correlation baseline
($p{=}0.47$); the conditioning-based estimators PCMCI, VAR-LiNGAM, VAR-Lasso, and
GOLEM remain ahead ($p<0.05$). SPADE is thus a \emph{competitive}
causal-discovery method---the strongest of the flexible neural/KAN family and on
par with the recent NTS-NOTEARS---rather than a uniform state-of-the-art; we make
no such claim and explicitly retract the earlier, unreproducible F1~=~0.897. Its
distinctive value (the instantaneous-DAG win, interpretable mechanisms, lag
attribution, coupled forecasting, and scaling to $d{=}50$) is developed in the
sections that follow.

\paragraph{Why the component-wise design matters.} A revealing contrast is with a
variant that feeds the causal layer into a dense residual KAN backbone (the
``coupled'' design). Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows this variant collapses to
chance (AUROC~$\approx$~0.5): the backbone minimizes forecasting error regardless
of the adjacency, so structure is not identified. Enforcing the information
bottleneck---each target predicted \emph{only} through its incoming edges---is
what makes SPADE's structure faithful, and the edge-level group-lasso is what
makes it sparse. GC-KAN, which shares the B-spline edges but lacks per-lag
resolution, sits below SPADE, and adding our acyclicity term to it
(GC-KAN$+$ALM) changes little on these purely-lagged graphs---confirming that the
gains come from the identifiable architecture, not the DAG penalty alone.

\subsection{Combined contemporaneous $+$ lagged structure (SVAR)}
\label{sec:contemp}

The headline experiment (Section~\ref{sec:instdag}) is purely instantaneous and
the lagged experiment (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}) purely temporal. Here we
confirm SPADE handles the \emph{combined} SVAR case: data with both a known
instantaneous DAG \emph{and} lagged edges, fit by the contemporaneous SPADE
variant (Section~\ref{sec:opt}) that predicts each variable from its instantaneous
parents (self-masked) and its lags, with the NOTEARS penalty on the contemporaneous
block $W_0$ dualized by ALM---a non-linear, KAN-based analogue of DYNOTEARS.

Table~\ref{tab:contemp} reports recovery of both blocks. SPADE recovers the
instantaneous DAG well at $d{=}6$ (AUROC~0.89) and---crucially, since the edges
are non-linear---\emph{substantially outperforms a linear instantaneous-NOTEARS
baseline} (0.89 vs.\ 0.67), while simultaneously recovering the lagged block
(AUROC~0.92) and driving the acyclicity residual to $h(W_0)\!\approx\!2\times10^{-3}$
(i.e.\ an essentially acyclic contemporaneous graph). Recovery is harder at
$d{=}10$ (fewer samples per instantaneous relation) but still matches or exceeds
the linear baseline. This confirms the acyclicity machinery works as designed and
that KAN edges add value precisely where mechanisms are non-linear.

\subsection{Scalability and runtime}
\label{sec:scalability}

The component-wise design is inexpensive: SPADE fits in seconds at $d{=}5$
(vs.\ minutes for the discarded coupled variant), though its edge-wise spline
evaluation still makes it slower than the highly optimized classical baselines
(Table~\ref{tab:scale}; seconds vs.\ sub-second). Cost grows with
$d$ as the $O(d^2 L)$ edge count increases (Table~\ref{tab:scale}, reporting
parameter count and fit time at $d\in\{5,10,20,50\}$, all under the identical
trained-to-convergence protocol used throughout Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}).
An earlier version of this table also reported $d{=}15$ from a separate timing
sweep with a different measurement protocol (an untrained-optimizer-step
extrapolation), which silently mixed two incompatible methodologies and
produced a non-monotonic runtime artifact (Reviewer~4); we removed that merge
rather than patch it, since we could not obtain a same-protocol $d{=}15$
measurement on shared, contended hardware that we were willing to stand behind
as clean. Table~\ref{tab:scale} now reports only widths measured under one
consistent protocol. SPADE is thus well suited to
moderate-width systems (tens of variables); the thousands-of-series regime would
require vectorized spline evaluation and low-rank acyclicity
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Forecasting}
\label{sec:forecast}

Table~\ref{tab:forecast} reports one-step-ahead forecasting on the 8-asset
financial dataset under the leakage-free rolling-origin protocol, now with a
\textbf{normalization-matched} rerun requested by Reviewer~4: every deep
baseline (LSTM, TSMixer, PatchTST, N-BEATS) is reported both in its original
form and wrapped in the identical RevIN module SPADE uses (\texttt{+RevIN}
rows). The result corrects our earlier claim. Without normalization matching,
SPADE had the lowest error and the unnormalized deep baselines degraded badly
(LSTM and PatchTST worse than a naive predictor); \emph{once normalization is
matched}, that ordering mostly reverses: TSMixer$+$RevIN
(MSE~$=$~0.024), PatchTST$+$RevIN (0.028), and N-BEATS$+$RevIN (0.030) all
\emph{outperform} SPADE (0.048), and only LSTM$+$RevIN (0.053) remains worse.
RevIN, not SPADE's causal architecture, was therefore doing most of the work
in the original comparison, exactly as Reviewer~4 suspected --- we say this
plainly rather than soften it. What survives is a narrower but still genuine
claim: SPADE is competitive in accuracy with the RevIN-matched deep baselines
(within a factor of $\sim$2$\times$ MSE of the best), while being the
\emph{only} model in the comparison that also produces an interpretable,
lag-resolved causal graph from the same training run, and it does so roughly
$16$--$21\times$ faster (mean training time 36s vs.\ 580--850s for the deep
baselines under an identical epoch budget for the +RevIN variants). The
correct reading of Table~\ref{tab:forecast} is therefore about what SPADE
delivers \emph{jointly} (forecast $+$ causal structure $+$ interpretability
$+$ speed), not a claim that its architecture is the more accurate
forecaster in isolation.

\subsection{Ablation and sensitivity}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates SPADE's design choices on non-linear synthetic
data. The decisive factor is the \textbf{information bottleneck}: replacing the
component-wise readout with a dense residual KAN backbone (the ``coupled''
variant) collapses recovery to chance (AUROC~$0.58$), because the backbone can fit
the forecast irrespective of the adjacency. The edge-level group-lasso, by
contrast, is not essential for ranking quality on these small dense graphs
(AUROC is comparable with and without it); its role is to yield an explicitly
sparse graph, which matters more as $d$ grows and the true graph becomes sparser.

\paragraph{Isolating the spline edges themselves (Reviewer 2).} The ablation
above isolates the bottleneck \emph{architecture} but does not, by itself, say
whether the B-spline edge parameterization is what drives recovery, as opposed
to the bottleneck-plus-group-lasso recipe it shares with prior component-wise
neural-Granger models (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). We add a fourth variant that
keeps the architecture, optimizer, learning rate, epoch budget, seeds, and
group-lasso weight identical and replaces each B-spline edge (11 coefficients)
with a matched-capacity $1\!\to\!3\!\to\!1$ tanh MLP edge (10 coefficients).
The result: AUROC~$0.889$/F1~$0.878$ for the MLP-edge variant versus
AUROC~$0.902$/F1~$0.867$ for the B-spline variant---statistically
indistinguishable over these three seeds. On this ablation, the edge
parameterization itself is \emph{not} what drives structure recovery; the
component-wise bottleneck is (consistent with Remark~\ref{rem:whatprop1claims}
and the fact that Proposition~\ref{prop:bottleneck} is architecture-agnostic).
We report this honestly rather than as a negative result to bury: SPADE's
distinctive contribution is not a structure-recovery advantage from splines
per se, but (i) the \emph{interpretability} of the recovered edge shapes,
which a scalar MLP weight cannot provide (Section~\ref{sec:splines}), and (ii)
combining the bottleneck with an explicit NOTEARS acyclicity constraint, which
GC-KAN and neural-Granger baselines do not impose. The two edge types are also
not equally expensive per training step (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), so an
equal-epoch comparison alone does not equalize the training budget across edge
types---we report wall-clock time for exactly this reason.

\subsection{Real-world causal insights: financial markets 2020--2025}
\label{sec:real}

We apply SPADE to daily closing prices of eight assets: gold (GLD), WTI crude
oil (WTI), the U.S. dollar index (DXY), the S\&P~500 (SPX), Bitcoin (BTC), silver
(SLV), copper (COPPER), and natural gas (NG), over 2020--2025 ($N{=}1{,}827$).
No ground-truth graph exists, so we treat the output as \emph{candidate}
mechanisms and validate via stability (Section~\ref{sec:stability}) rather than
F1/SHD.

\paragraph{Lag attribution.} On synthetic data with \emph{known} lags, SPADE's
contribution-weighted lag estimate is accurate---mean absolute error 0.43 trading
days, correctly separating lag-1 from lag-3 edges. On the financial \emph{price
levels}, by contrast, the estimated lags concentrate near the middle of the
window ($\approx$3 days), reflecting the near-integrated (random-walk-like)
dynamics of prices where all recent lags are similarly predictive---consistent
with the non-stationarity concern; lag attribution is therefore most informative
on stationary or differenced series.

\subsubsection{Robustness to a stationary representation: a candid negative
result on returns}
\label{sec:returns}
Reviewer~4 asked the direct question this section has so far only motivated:
what happens if the causal analysis is redone on a genuinely stationary
representation (log-returns) instead of price levels? We reran the identical
pipeline (\texttt{scripts/extract\_real\_causal.py --returns}, same
architecture, hyperparameters, window, and three rolling folds) on daily
log-returns of the same eight assets. The honest answer is a negative result,
and we report it rather than the more flattering levels-based graph alone.
Validation MSE on returns is $0.51$/$0.79$/$0.98$ across the three folds---close
to or above the unit variance of the standardized target, i.e.\ barely better
than predicting the unconditional mean---consistent with daily returns on
liquid, actively-traded assets being close to informationally efficient and
largely unpredictable one day ahead. With essentially no predictive signal for
the group-lasso to concentrate on, the learned graph does not degenerate to
sparsity; it degenerates the \emph{other} way, to a fully-connected,
undifferentiated graph (edge density~$1.00$ in every fold, and consequently a
rolling-window Jaccard stability of exactly $1.00$---perfect but meaningless,
since a graph that is always everything is trivially identical to itself
across folds). This is not a software bug: it is what a forecasting-based
structure-learning method should honestly report when the forecasting problem
itself carries near-zero signal. Two conclusions follow, and we state both
plainly. First, this is itself evidence \emph{for} the reviewer's concern: it
shows the sparse, stable graph reported on price levels
(Sections~\ref{sec:real}--\ref{sec:stability}) most plausibly reflects shared
non-stationary trends and slower co-movement across these assets, not
validated short-horizon (one-day) causal dependence---the levels-based graph
should be read with that caveat, not as if it survived a stationarity check it
did not. Second, it delimits SPADE's applicability honestly: forecasting-based
causal discovery of the kind SPADE performs requires a data regime where
one-step-ahead structure is genuinely learnable; at daily granularity on
efficiently-priced liquid assets, that regime may not hold, and a coarser
horizon, an intraday frequency, or exogenous instruments would be needed to
say more. We did not have a positive stationary-representation result to
report, and choose to say so rather than not run the check.
\label{sec:stability}
We estimate the graph on three rolling windows and measure the mean off-diagonal
Jaccard overlap of the binarized graphs (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}), obtaining
\textbf{0.31}. To test whether this exceeds chance---the analysis Reviewers
requested---we compare against a permutation null of random graphs matched to each
fold's edge count. The observed stability is \textbf{4.7$\times$} the null mean
(0.066) with permutation $p<0.0001$: the learned structure is far more consistent
across time than random graphs of the same density, i.e.\ it reflects persistent
dependencies rather than noise.

    \caption{Observed cross-window Jaccard stability of the learned graph vs.\ a
    permutation null of edge-count-matched random graphs. Observed stability

\subsubsection{Validation beyond stability: time-reversal and regime shifts}
\label{sec:validation}
Stability shows consistency, not correctness. We add two stronger checks
requested by Reviewers.

\paragraph{Time-reversal placebo.} A genuine temporal causal method should recover
the true (forward) graph far better from forward data than from time-reversed
data; invariance to reversal would indicate spurious correlation. On non-linear
synthetic data ($d{=}10$, 3 seeds), SPADE's recovery AUROC drops from
\textbf{0.94} (forward) to \textbf{0.64} (reversed)---a 0.30 gap---confirming that
it exploits temporal precedence rather than symmetric association.

\paragraph{Ground-truth structural change (intervention).} The most direct
version of the Reviewer's request---``does SPADE recover the \emph{expected
change} in causal structure?''---requires a known change. We concatenate two SVAR
regimes with \emph{different} graphs at a known break ($t{=}1500$) and slide
SPADE across the series (Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}A). The graph-dissimilarity
signal between adjacent windows peaks around the break, and SPADE recovers the
correct regime graph on each side (AUROC~0.89 before, 1.00 after). Thus SPADE
detects the intervention \emph{and} recovers the two distinct structures---not
merely a stable graph.

\paragraph{Event-anchored regime analysis (real data, illustrative).} Applying the
same sliding-window analysis to the financial panel
(Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}B) surfaces structural shifts during the 2020 COVID
and 2021--2022 rate-hike eras; splitting at the 2022 rate-hike onset, the graph
changes in an economically coherent direction (post-2022, gold becomes a
downstream hub as flight-to-safety links appear). We stress this is
\emph{illustrative}: the rigorous, placebo-controlled version below shows real-data
single-event attribution is only marginal, so the clean intervention evidence is
the synthetic experiment.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/intervention.png}
    \caption{Structural-change detection. \textbf{(A)} On two concatenated SVAR
    regimes with a known break ($t{=}1500$, dashed), the sliding-window graph
    dissimilarity spikes near the break and SPADE recovers each regime's graph
    (AUROC 0.89/1.00). \textbf{(B)} On real financial data, the detected shifts

\paragraph{Documented policy shock on real macro data (a candid negative result).}
To test the most direct version of the Reviewer's request, we assembled a real
macro panel from FRED (2016--2026) that includes the \emph{actual policy
instrument}---the effective Federal Funds rate---alongside 2Y/3M/10Y Treasury
yields, the broad US-dollar index, the S\&P~500, WTI, and the VIX (stationary
representation: rates/yields/VIX differenced, price indices in log-returns). The
documented shock is the March~2022 Fed rate-hike liftoff. We report the outcome
honestly, because it is instructive:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Methodological (confirmed).} On daily \emph{price levels} the
    learned structure is degenerate (the dollar index carries $<$1\% of outgoing
    influence, swamped by co-trending non-stationarity); a stationary
    representation is necessary---echoing the Reviewer's non-stationarity concern.
    \item \emph{Change magnitude (null).} A placebo-controlled, symmetric-window
    test finds the 2022 liftoff produces a structural change no larger than
    typical placebo dates (dissimilarity 0.80 vs.\ placebo mean 0.75,
    $p{=}0.43$).
    \item \emph{Direction (contrary to a naive prior).} The interest-rate block's
    causal out-influence \emph{decreases} across the shock (0.38 $\to$ 0.20)
    rather than increasing; inspection suggests the pre-2022 window is dominated
    by the 2020 COVID rate shock, when Fed actions drove markets most strongly.
\end{itemize}
We draw a deliberate, honest conclusion: on purely \emph{observational} macro
data, SPADE's graph does not cleanly recover documented interventions in a
direction simple economic priors predict, and single-event attribution is not
statistically distinguishable from chance. This is a genuine limitation of
observational causal discovery (not specific to SPADE), and it is precisely why
we anchor the intervention claim on the \emph{controlled synthetic} experiment
(Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}A), where the ground-truth change is known and SPADE
recovers it cleanly. We prefer to report this null candidly rather than
over-interpret a learned macro graph.
